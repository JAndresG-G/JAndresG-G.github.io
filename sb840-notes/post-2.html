SB 840 Research Notes - Post 2

Where Does SB 840 Actually Apply?

SB 840 is a statewide law, but it does not create the same opening on every parcel or in every city. To measure where it applies, we had to translate a state statute through 18 local zoning systems.

← Housing context Post 2 of 3 Likely conversion →

SB 840 sounds broad because it changes what large Texas cities can require on many nonresidential parcels. But the legal geography is narrower and messier than the statute alone suggests.

The first post in this series started with the housing context: Texas has built a lot of homes through outward growth, but rising affordability pressure and long-run infrastructure costs have pushed the state toward housing reforms that make more use of already-urbanized land.

This post asks a narrower question: where does SB 840 actually apply?

That sounds simple. It is not. SB 840 is a statewide law, but it works through local zoning systems. Texas cities do not use one zoning vocabulary. A district called "commercial" in one city may allow a different set of uses than a district with a similar name somewhere else. Some cities use mixed-use districts extensively; others rely on planned development districts that have to be interpreted ordinance by ordinance.

The basic rule

SB 840 applies to Texas municipalities with more than 150,000 residents that are wholly or partly located in a county with more than 300,000 residents. When the law took effect on September 1, 2025, 19 cities met that population test. Our parcel analysis covers 18 of them. Houston is the missing city, not because it is unimportant, but because it does not have a formal zoning ordinance for SB 840 to operate through.

Within covered cities, SB 840 focuses on parcels in zoning classifications that allow office, commercial, retail, warehouse, or mixed-use activity. On qualifying parcels, the law can require cities to treat multifamily or mixed-use residential projects as permitted uses by right. It also limits several local development standards for qualifying projects, including some density, height, parking, floor-area, and discretionary approval requirements.

The result is a funnel. Across the 18-city study area, the broad office, commercial, retail, warehouse, and mixed-use footprint that SB 840 could potentially affect is about 417 square miles. Statutory and legal exclusions remove about 31.7 percent of that broad potentially affected area. After those exclusions, the legally eligible footprint is about 285 square miles.

From statute to map categories

To make the law mappable, we created city-specific zoning dictionaries. Each dictionary takes the zoning categories used by a city and assigns them to a smaller set of SB 840 categories.

Map category What it means
New multifamily or mixed-use opportunity SB 840 appears to newly authorize multifamily or mixed-use housing in a district where it was not previously allowed under the local code.
Greater density or reduced requirements Multifamily or mixed-use housing was already partly allowed, but SB 840 may increase density, height, or feasibility by limiting local requirements.
Review needed Planned development, special district, ambiguous zoning code, or another case where public attributes do not allow a confident classification.

In the underlying data, the first two categories are the categories we often refer to as Effect 3 and Effect 4. The labels are useful shorthand for the analysis, but the public interpretation is more intuitive: one category marks newly opened residential opportunities, and the other marks places where the law may loosen requirements for housing that was already partly allowed.

Why zoning dictionaries matter

State preemption is often described as if state law simply overrides local zoning. Legally, SB 840 does preempt some local authority. Empirically, however, we still have to know what local zoning says in the first place.

Imagine two cities with a district labeled "C-2." In one city, C-2 might allow office and retail but not residential. In another, it might already allow mixed-use development. In a third city, the comparable district might be called "General Commercial" or "Neighborhood Services" or something else entirely. The label alone is not enough.

Method in plain language: for each city, we used the zoning GIS layer, the zoning ordinance or development code, and a city-specific zoning dictionary. The dictionary is the bridge between statewide legal language and local zoning vocabulary.

Where judgment enters

The zoning dictionaries are not just a clerical step. They are an interpretive layer, and some local codes create choices that reasonable readers might resolve differently.

Frisco is a good example. Its zoning ordinance use chart allows "government offices" in every zoning classification. One possible legal argument is that if offices are allowed everywhere, then a literal reading of SB 840's office-language could make almost the entire city eligible. We did not use that interpretation. Instead, we treated "government offices" as a special civic use and classified Frisco districts according to the broader purpose, use mix, and regulatory intent of each district.

Austin creates a different kind of problem. SB 840 preserves local authority over historic districts and landmarks, but local preservation intent does not always appear in zoning data as a simple "historic district" flag. Austin's Neighborhood Conservation Combining Districts are meant to preserve places with distinctive neighborhood character and architectural patterns. We treated those districts as legal exclusions for this analysis, while recognizing that future implementation, administrative guidance, or litigation could draw that boundary differently.

Dallas shows why this gets hard

Dallas is the clearest example of why SB 840 cannot be mapped by zoning label alone. Large parts of Dallas are governed by planned development districts. A planned development district is not one standardized use category. It is often a negotiated district with its own ordinance, use list, development standards, and site-specific conditions.

That creates a real measurement problem. In the current Dallas data, more than 55,000 parcels are both zoned planned development and placed in the review-needed category. That represents roughly three-quarters of Dallas's affected-or-review universe. Dallas provides PD numbers, ordinance numbers, and case fields, but not a single clean base-zoning field that can be used consistently across the city to determine what each parcel allows.

What this stage establishes

The key finding from this stage is not just that SB 840 applies to less land than a broad reading might imply. The deeper point is that legal geography has to be constructed.

Once that work is done, the footprint is narrower than the language of the law. The broad potentially affected geography is about 417 square miles across the 18 study cities. After statutory and legal exclusions, about 285 square miles remain legally eligible.

That legal footprint is the starting point, not the conclusion. The next question is what part of that legally eligible geography looks plausible for actual redevelopment or conversion.
