SB 840 Research Notes - Post 1

What Could SB 840 Actually Change?

Texas has built a lot of housing by growing outward. SB 840 raises a more empirical question: how much room for housing exists inside the commercial and mixed-use land Texas cities have already urbanized?

← Previous post Post 1 of 3 Legal geography →

SB 840 is best understood as an empirical question, not a simple verdict on whether Texas should sprawl. The law creates new legal possibilities, but the public question is where those possibilities exist and whether they are likely to become actual housing.

In April 2025, Conor Dougherty published a New York Times Magazine essay titled "Why America Should Sprawl." The Texas example behind the essay was not imaginary. Compared with many high-cost coastal metros, Texas metropolitan regions have generally allowed more outward growth, and the Dallas-Fort Worth region in particular has produced housing at a scale that many slower-growth regions have not matched.

That is important context, not a verdict. Sprawl can produce homes. It can also create long-term costs that are easy to understate when the only thing being counted is the number of new units delivered this year.

Our interest in SB 840 is not to decide, in the abstract, whether the bill is good or bad. The more useful empirical question is what the law might actually mean on the ground. Where does it apply? Which parcels does it plausibly affect? Which of those parcels look like realistic near-term conversion candidates once existing uses, ownership, land values, and market conditions are considered?

The reason those questions matter is that Texas is facing two housing problems at once. The first is the familiar affordability problem: home prices and the income needed to buy a typical home have risen faster than many household incomes. The second is an urban-form problem: continuing to solve housing demand mainly through low-density outward expansion creates future obligations for cities that are not always visible at the moment of subdivision approval.

The major Texas metros are still less expensive than places like Los Angeles, San Francisco, Seattle, Boston, and New York. That cost advantage is one reason Texas has been able to attract households, workers, and firms. But the advantage has narrowed in the state's largest growth markets. Dallas-Fort Worth is no longer a cheap housing market in any ordinary sense. Austin's recent price run-up made it look, for a time, more like a national high-cost metro than the older Texas affordability story would suggest. Houston remains the most important within-Texas comparison case, partly because it lacks a formal zoning code, but even Houston is not immune from affordability pressure.

Home prices alone do not tell the whole affordability story. Mortgage rates turn a sales price into a monthly payment, and a monthly payment determines which households can actually buy. The Zillow income-needed series is useful because it translates price and financing conditions into a buyer-facing measure: how much income a household would need to purchase a typical home with a 20 percent down payment.

The fiscal side of sprawl

The fiscal side of sprawl is less visible in most housing debates, but it is central to why a state might want cities to make better use of already-urbanized land. Low-density development requires roads, water lines, sewer lines, drainage systems, fire protection, policing, emergency medical response, parks, and ongoing maintenance across a large area. The tax base created by low-density housing often does not generate enough revenue relative to the land and infrastructure it requires to cover those long-run service obligations.

Cities can manage that gap for a while by relying on growth, issuing debt, delaying maintenance, or shifting costs across the rest of the tax base. But deferred maintenance does not disappear. It becomes a future tradeoff between public safety and financial solvency: deteriorated roads, bridges, water systems, and drainage infrastructure eventually have to be repaired, replaced, or allowed to fail.

The 2025 housing package

This is the policy context for the 2025 Texas housing package. The state did not pass one housing bill. It passed a group of bills aimed at different parts of the housing-production problem: small lots, rezoning protests, commercial-to-residential reuse, office conversions, apartment building design, manufactured housing, and occupancy restrictions.

Those bills are not universally popular. Many local officials, neighborhood groups, and residents object to state preemption of zoning and land-use authority. Their concerns are not all the same. Some are about local democratic control. Some are about infrastructure capacity, design standards, displacement, neighborhood change, traffic, parking, or the loss of a city's ability to negotiate with developers.

Bill What it does in plain English Why it belongs in this series
S.B. 840 Lets qualifying multifamily and mixed-use housing be built on many parcels already zoned for office, commercial, retail, warehouse, or mixed-use activity in large Texas cities. This is the bill our parcel analysis maps and models.
S.B. 15 Allows smaller starter-home lots on certain large, unplatted single-family tracts in large and midsize cities. Shows that the 2025 package also targeted lower-cost ownership forms.
H.B. 24 Changes protest rules for rezoning. Helps explain the procedural-veto problem that SB 840 also responds to.
S.B. 2477 Makes it easier to convert certain office buildings into apartments or mixed-use residential buildings. Works alongside SB 840's broader commercial-conversion framework.
S.B. 2835 Allows cities to permit small apartment buildings with one stairway if they meet size and fire-safety conditions. Targets construction costs and building form, especially on smaller urban parcels.
S.B. 785 Requires municipalities to allow new HUD-code manufactured homes by right in at least one residential zoning category or district, subject to exceptions. Expands a lower-cost ownership option outside the SB 840 parcel universe.
S.B. 1567 Limits local restrictions on unrelated people sharing homes in certain college-town municipalities. Shows the package also tried to use existing homes more efficiently.

From state law to parcel geography

The law does not abolish zoning. It does not open single-family neighborhoods to apartment development. It applies to specific cities, specific zoning categories, specific project types, and specific legal conditions. It also contains exclusions for places such as airports, military areas, heavy industrial zones, and historic districts.

Across the 18 eligible Texas cities in our parcel analysis, we translated SB 840's legal language into a parcel-level geography. We identified areas where the law appears to create completely new opportunities for multifamily or mixed-use housing by right, and locations where the law may allow greater density or reduce other requirements for multifamily or mixed-use housing that was already partly allowed.

The first result is that SB 840 is sweeping in language but narrower in practice. Across the 18-city study area, our processed parcel universe covers roughly 2,470 square miles. Of that total, about 16.9 percent is in the broad set of office, commercial, retail, warehouse, and mixed-use zoning contexts that SB 840 could potentially affect before statutory exclusions are applied. The legally eligible footprint becomes smaller still after statutory and legal exclusions are applied.

The second result is even more important: legal eligibility is not the same thing as likely redevelopment. Parcels have owners, tenants, buildings, leases, public functions, infrastructure constraints, and market conditions. A parcel can be legally eligible for multifamily development and still be a poor candidate for near-term conversion.

This first post sets the housing and fiscal context. The second post turns to SB 840 itself and asks where the law actually applies. The third post asks what is most likely to happen on the ground once legal eligibility, parcel conditions, and financial feasibility are considered together.

The point is not to treat SB 840 as a cure-all or a catastrophe. The point is to measure the gap between what the statute permits and what the urban land market is likely to do with that permission.
